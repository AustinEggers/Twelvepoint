Flood zone versus flood history

Ask about flooding in Houston and you will usually be told a flood zone letter. That letter is a real answer to a real question — it just is not the question most buyers think they are asking.

A FEMA flood zone is a regulatory designation. It drives whether your lender requires flood insurance and roughly what that costs. It is drawn from modelling and historical data, updated periodically, and it describes probability across an area.

Whether this specific house has ever taken water is a separate matter, and the map will not tell you.

Why the two come apart

Houston is flat, drains slowly, and gets rain in quantities that do not care much about the modelling. Homes outside mapped high-risk zones have flooded. Homes inside them have stayed dry for decades. Local drainage, the elevation of the lot relative to its street, and what was built upstream since the map was drawn all matter enormously and none of them are in the letter.

The zone tells you what the insurance will cost. The history tells you what happened.

Asking the second question

Texas requires sellers to disclose known flooding and previous flood-related claims on the seller's disclosure notice. Read that section properly rather than skimming it — it is one of the more useful parts of the form.

Beyond it:

  • Ask for the elevation certificate if one exists. It records how high the structure sits relative to the base flood elevation, and it is the most concrete document in this whole conversation.
  • Ask the neighbours. Genuinely. People who have lived on a street through a storm will tell you exactly which houses took water.
  • Ask about the claim history rather than only about flooding. A house can have been flooded without a claim, and a claim history follows the property.
  • Look at the lot, not just the map. Where does water go when it leaves this property, and what is uphill of it?

Insurance is not only for high-risk zones

Lenders require flood insurance in high-risk zones. Nobody prevents you from buying it elsewhere, and outside those zones it is usually inexpensive. Standard homeowner policies do not cover flood — that surprises people at the worst possible moment.

Given how much Houston flooding has happened outside mapped high-risk areas, treating the requirement as the decision is a mistake worth avoiding.

The short version

Get the zone, then get the history. Read the disclosure, ask for an elevation certificate, and talk to someone on the street. Then decide about insurance on the facts rather than on whether a lender made you.

Flood maps are revised and drainage projects change conditions. Confirm the current designation for any specific address, and treat this as background rather than a substitute for the disclosure and an insurance quote.
