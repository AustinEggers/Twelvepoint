A new listing is at its most valuable in its first fortnight. Everyone waiting on a home in that area sees it at once, saved searches fire, and the people who have been looking for months finally have something new to argue about. That attention does not come back.

Price is what decides whether you convert it.

Why aiming high costs money

The theory is reasonable: start above the number, leave room to negotiate, come down if nothing happens. The theory ignores what has already been spent.

Buyers who saw it in week one at the higher price mostly do not come back for the reduction. They have moved on. So the price cut is shown to a smaller, later audience than the original number was — you have discounted to fewer people, not more.

Days on market is the number buyers ask about second, immediately after price.

Meanwhile the days-on-market count is running. Past a certain point buyers stop asking what is wrong with the price and start asking what is wrong with the house. Two price reductions and forty days tell a story whether or not it is true, and you end up negotiating from inside that story.

What the market is telling you

The feedback arrives faster than most sellers expect, and it is fairly legible:

  • Showings but no offers — the photographs are working and the house is not. Usually condition, layout, or something on the lot.
  • No showings at all — almost always price. Buyers filter by number before they ever see the pictures.
  • Strong traffic, low offers — the market has an opinion about the number and it is consistent.

Ten showings and no offer is not bad luck. It is ten separate people reaching the same conclusion.

Adjust decisively or not at all

If the number needs to move, move it far enough to reach a new set of buyers. Small reductions burn weeks and reach essentially the same audience that already declined. A cut that clears the next search bracket puts the house in front of people who have never seen it.

The short version

Price it to sell in the first two weeks. If it does not, read the feedback honestly and move once, properly — rather than three times, slowly.
