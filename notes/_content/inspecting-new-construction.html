The most common thing said to a buyer about inspecting a new home is that there is no point. It is new. It passed inspection. It comes with a warranty.

All three are true and none of them mean what people take them to mean.

What the city inspection actually covers

A municipal inspection checks compliance with building code at particular stages. It is a pass or fail against a minimum standard, performed quickly, on a lot of houses. It is not a quality review and it is not looking out for you.

A house can meet code and still have a drain that was never connected, insulation missing from a section of attic, a bathroom fan venting into the roof space, or grading that sends water toward the slab. Those are workmanship, not code, and nobody has been paid to find them on your behalf.

Everything gets built by people, at pace, in the Texas heat. New does not mean flawless — it means the problems have not surfaced yet.

Inspect at two points, not one

Before drywall. This is the one most buyers never think to ask for and the one with the highest return. Framing, plumbing, electrical and ductwork are all visible for a short window and invisible forever afterwards. Finding a problem here means it gets fixed; finding it later means opening a wall.

Not every builder permits it and some require a scheduled visit with someone present. Ask early rather than assuming.

Before closing. The full inspection, done the way you would on a resale, before your final walkthrough — so the walkthrough list is built from a report rather than from what you happen to spot in an hour.

Read the warranty, not the brochure

The standard most builders offer is one, two, ten — one year on workmanship and materials, two years on plumbing, electrical, heating and air conditioning, and ten years on major structural components. That is what you should expect to see.

There is now a reason to confirm it rather than assume it. Texas changed the law in 2023, and the change created a trade the builder gets to make.

The statute of repose — the outer deadline for bringing a construction defect claim at all — stays at ten years by default. But a builder who provides a written warranty of at least one, two and six can rely on a shortened six-year repose period instead.

So a builder offering the full 1-2-10 is giving you more than the minimum. A builder offering 1-2-6 has bought themselves a shorter window in which you can bring a claim.

Both are legal. Which one you were handed is written in your warranty document and nowhere else.

Check the structural number specifically. Then note what is excluded, and what you have to do to make a claim — most warranties have requirements about notice and access that are easy to breach by accident.

Claiming is a process with rules

Construction defect disputes in Texas run through the Residential Construction Liability Act, which requires written notice to the builder and a defined opportunity for them to inspect and offer a repair before you can sue. That is not a formality to skip — the sequence matters.

Two practical consequences:

  • Put everything in writing. A conversation with a site manager is not a warranty claim. Email is.
  • Diary the end of each warranty period and do a proper walk of the house a month or two before it lapses.

The short version

Inspect before drywall if the builder allows it, inspect again before closing, read the warranty rather than the brochure, and put every claim in writing.

Warranty terms and the law behind them have both changed recently, and this is general information rather than legal advice. For a specific defect, talk to a construction attorney — and read your own warranty document first.
