Every seller asks some version of the same question: what should I fix before we list? The honest answer is that most improvements do not return what they cost, and a few are worth doing precisely because skipping them costs you more.

The distinction is not about how much a job improves the house. It is about what a buyer does when they see it undone.

Fix the things that stop a buyer

Some conditions do not reduce an offer — they prevent one. A buyer who sees active water staining, a visibly failing roof, or a foundation with obvious movement does not mentally deduct the repair cost. They move to the next house, because unknown problems feel larger than known ones.

Anything that reads as "there may be more going on here" belongs in the fix column:

  • Active leaks and the stains they leave
  • Electrical faults a buyer can see — a panel with obvious issues, dead outlets
  • Roof damage visible from the street
  • Anything a lender is likely to require before funding

Do the cheap cosmetic work

Paint, deep cleaning, decluttering and landscaping are the highest-return money in the whole exercise, and it is not close. They cost hundreds rather than thousands, and they change the photographs — which is where nearly every buyer forms their opinion before they ever stand in the room.

Buyers are not appraisers. They react to what they see, then look for reasons to justify the reaction.

Think hard before the big remodel

A full kitchen or bathroom renovation immediately before listing rarely pays for itself. You spend at current labour prices, you choose finishes to your taste rather than the market's, and you hand the buyer something they may have wanted to choose themselves.

A dated but clean and functional kitchen sells. A half-finished one does not. If the choice is between a big remodel and pricing to reflect the kitchen, pricing usually wins.

The one that is nearly always worth it

A pre-listing inspection. Not because the report is pleasant, but because it moves the surprises to a moment when you still have options. Finding a problem while you are on the market means renegotiating from a weak position, usually under a deadline. Finding it beforehand means getting quotes calmly, fixing it or pricing it deliberately, and being able to hand a buyer the paperwork.

What Texas requires either way

You have to disclose known material defects on the seller's disclosure notice. Learning about a problem does not create an obligation to repair it — but it does create one to disclose it. That is a reason to know early, not a reason to avoid looking.

The short version

Fix what frightens a buyer. Clean and paint everything. Leave the remodel to whoever buys it.
