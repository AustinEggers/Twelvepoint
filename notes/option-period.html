What the option period actually buys you

Buying August 2026 5 min read

The option period is the most useful thing in a Texas contract and the most commonly wasted. For a small fee and a short window — usually somewhere between three and ten days — you buy an unrestricted right to terminate. Not a right to terminate if the inspection is bad. Any reason. No reason.

That is genuinely unusual, and it is worth understanding before you are sitting in it.

How the money works

Two separate payments leave your hands early, and people mix them up constantly.

  • The option fee buys the right to walk away. It is credited toward the purchase price at closing, so if you buy the house you effectively get it back.
  • The earnest money is your deposit showing you are serious. It is also credited at closing, but it is at risk if you walk away after the option period ends.

Both now go to the title company rather than to the seller, and both have to be delivered within three days of the effective date. That deadline is not a formality. Miss it and you can lose the option entirely — the thing you paid for — while still being bound to buy the house.

The option period is a clock, and it starts whether or not you have booked anything.

The mistake almost everyone makes

They treat day one as a rest day. The contract gets executed on a Friday, everyone exhales, and the inspector gets called on Monday. That is two days of a seven-day window gone before anything has been scheduled.

A good inspector in Houston can be booked out. The report takes a day or two to arrive after the walk. If you want a foundation engineer or a roofer to look at something the inspector flagged, that is another appointment on top. The arithmetic runs out faster than people expect.

What the first 24 hours should look like

  • Inspection booked, ideally for the next available slot
  • Option fee and earnest money delivered to the title company
  • Lender told the contract is executed so the appraisal can be ordered
  • A note in your calendar for the exact hour the option expires

What "any reason" really means

You can terminate because the inspection worried you. You can also terminate because you drove the neighborhood at 10pm and did not like it, because the commute felt longer than you thought, or because you simply changed your mind. Nobody is owed an explanation.

What you cannot do is terminate late. The deadline is a specific date and time, and it does not stretch because the report was slow or because you were waiting on a contractor to call back. If the answer is not clear and the clock is nearly out, an extension has to be negotiated and paid for before it expires — not after.

The short version

Treat the option period as a countdown that started the moment the contract was signed. Book the inspection first and celebrate afterwards.

TREC revises its contract forms periodically, and the option provisions have changed more than once in recent years. Anything in this note is general information rather than legal advice — ask us, or your attorney, about the version in front of you.
